A real run · nothing rewritten

Three AI companies. Three different answers.

We gave one small pool company’s problem to Google’s Gemini, OpenAI’s GPT and Anthropic’s Claude at the same moment, each sealed off from the other two. Then we showed them each other’s work with the names stripped off and asked them to mark it. Every one of the three drafts was rejected by somebody. Below is exactly what came back — word for word, nothing tidied up.

1 The brief

One page. All three got the same one.

No hints, no house style, no nudge toward an answer we already liked. Whatever separates the three below came from the models, not from us.

brief.txt — sent to all three, unchanged
A pool service company in Gilbert, Arizona. Two trucks, two techs plus the owner.
140 recurring accounts at $135/month. The owner answers the phone himself.
Most new leads call between 8am and 4pm while he is in a backyard with his arms in a pool,
so they go to voicemail, and by the time he calls back that evening they have already
booked whoever answered. He has tried Angi and hated the shared leads.
He wants more recurring accounts, not one-off cleanings.
"This worked" in 90 days = 20 new recurring accounts.
This is a composite Gilbert pool company, not a real client — the brief is ours. The three answers below are entirely theirs.
2 Three sealed passes

Same brief. Same minute. No contact.

They ran in parallel and none could see the others. Read the third column against the first two — that gap is the entire argument for paying three companies instead of one.

Google

Gemini 3.6 Flash

The Reader answered in 38.0s
The position it took
Present as Gilbert’s most reliable, neighborhood-dedicated pool care provider that guarantees instant communication and flawless weekly service.
The line it would put on the page
“Get your first month of reliable weekly pool service for 50% off.”
Why this and not the obvious answer
The root problem isn't generating interest, but losing high-intent callers to faster competitors while the owner is in the field. Pairing an automated missed-call text-back system with targeted local search ads captures exclusive recurring clients at the exact moment they are ready to buy.
How it says it could be wrong
If the owner resists adopting an automated instant-text response system, leads will continue booking whoever answers first regardless of the marketing offer.
OpenAI

Codex CLI · ChatGPT

The Maker answered in 14.8s
The position it took
Present as Gilbert’s responsive, route-based weekly pool-care specialist that makes switching effortless.
The line it would put on the page
“Switch to reliable weekly pool care—get a quote by text in 60 seconds.”
Why this and not the obvious answer
The immediate text response fixes the real conversion problem: missed calls, not insufficient demand. Emphasizing weekly care and switching filters out one-off shoppers without returning to shared-lead marketplaces.
How it says it could be wrong
This fails if lead volume is too low to produce 20 accounts even with faster conversion.
Anthropic

Claude Code CLI

The Builder & The Check answered in 31.1s
The position it took
A closed neighborhood route, not a service company that answers phones: a small, nearly-full outfit that takes a handful of homes per street, same named tech, same day every week — and the calendar, not the owner, closes the sale.
The line it would put on the page
“Same tech, same day every week. Pick your slot online. $135/month.”
Why this and not the obvious answer
The obvious angle is speed-to-lead — answer faster than whoever answered first — but that is plumbing, not a position, and it wins the same one-off price shopper Angi already burned him on. A bookable route slot makes the 8am voicemail irrelevant instead of merely faster, and scarcity framed as route capacity self-selects for people buying a standing weekly relationship rather than a cheap one-time cleaning.
How it says it could be wrong
If Gilbert callers at 8am are actually buying urgency rather than reliability, the scarcity frame reads as a gimmick and a plain missed-call text-back would out-convert online booking on the same leads.
3 Blind cross-review

Then they marked each other, names off.

Each model saw the other two drafts labelled only “Draft A” and “Draft B”, with no way to know who wrote what, and was told to judge only on whether it would actually win recurring accounts.

Three drafts. Three keeps. Three kills.
Not one of them survived untouched.

If all three had agreed, you would have learned nothing you could not get from one subscription. The disagreement is the product — and so is what they agreed on anyway.

Gemini’s draft

“Get your first month of reliable weekly pool service for 50% off.”

  • Claude kept it

    “A 50% off first month invites price-shoppers who churn at month two, which is the opposite of the 20 recurring accounts he needs, and it drops ~$67 per account without any commitment or switch-friendly framing to hold them.”

  • Codex killed it

    “A generic 50%-off first month attracts price shoppers, weakens trust, and does nothing to ensure the 20 wins are route-dense recurring accounts.”

Codex’s draft

“Switch to reliable weekly pool care—get a quote by text in 60 seconds.”

  • Gemini kept it

    “An instant text response mechanism must immediately qualify pool size and setup before confirming pricing, or the owner risks locking in underpriced accounts.”

  • Claude killed it

    “"Get a quote by text in 60 seconds" is a mechanic, not an offer — a pool owner already has a price in mind at $135, so nothing here gives them a reason to switch today, and it never says what happens after the text, which is where the recurring account is actually won.”

Claude’s draft

“Same tech, same day every week. Pick your slot online. $135/month.”

  • Codex kept it

    “Online booking must restrict availability by neighborhood and route capacity or scattered new accounts will destroy the operational advantage.”

  • Gemini killed it

    “Forcing self-service online calendar booking for weekly pool care fails because route density, pool volume, and equipment condition require owner pre-assessment before a spot can be assigned.”

Gemini would keep at all costs

“Automating an instant text-back response to capture leads before competitors can answer while strictly framing the offer around ongoing weekly service rather than one-time cleanings.”

Codex would keep at all costs

“Keep the real-time, route-qualified online slot booking that lets homeowners commit to $135 monthly service without waiting for the owner to call back.”

Claude would keep at all costs

“Draft A's missed-call text-back paired with local search ads — it fixes the exact loss point (high-intent callers hitting voicemail while he's in a backyard) within seconds rather than that evening, and it's the only piece in either draft that directly converts the demand he already has.”

4 The merge

What two out of three agreed on, survives.

This is the rule, applied to the run above — not a summary written afterwards to make it look tidy.

Survives · 3 of 3

Answer the caller in seconds, not that evening

Every seat, unprompted, put the missed 8am call at the centre. Two of the three named an instant text-back as the single idea they would keep at all costs. The third built its whole position around making the missed call irrelevant instead of merely faster.

This is the one thing nobody argued with.

Survives · 2 of 3

Qualify by route and pool before you quote

Two seats independently attacked the same blind spot from opposite directions: one warned that instant quoting locks in underpriced accounts before anyone knows the pool, the other that unrestricted online booking scatters accounts across town and destroys the route.

Neither draft contained this. It only exists because they read each other.

Cut · 2 of 3

The 50%-off first month

The most conventional idea in the run, and the one that got hit hardest. Two of the three said the same thing in different words — a half-price first month buys price shoppers who churn in month two, which is the exact opposite of twenty recurring accounts.

One model proposing it and two rejecting it is precisely the catch a single subscription cannot make.

Cut · 2 of 3

“Get a quote by text in 60 seconds”

Rejected as a mechanic dressed up as an offer: the homeowner already has a price in mind at $135, and the line never says what happens after the text — which is where the recurring account is actually won or lost.

It reads well. That is what makes it dangerous.

5 The part we did not plan

All three pointed at the same thing.

Nobody told them what we sell. They were given a pool company that loses callers to voicemail and asked what to do about it. Three competing AI companies, sealed off from each other, each arrived at the same root fix — answer the person the moment they call, before anyone else can.

That is the $497 product on this site.

An AI employee that answers every call, text and web form in about eight seconds, books the job, and follows up until they reply. We did not build it because three models agreed. They agreed with something already built — and this run is the first time we could show you that instead of just claiming it.
